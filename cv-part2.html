PART 2

Image Classification with a Simple CNN

Beginner-friendly · Convolutional Neural Networks · Deep Learning

In Part 1, we saw how computers represent images as grids of numbers. Now it's time to build the model that turns those numbers into a prediction: a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). We'll go layer by layer, from raw pixels to a class label like "cat" or "dog."

📸 What is image classification?

Image classification is the task of assigning a single label to an entire image. Show the model a photo, and it answers one question: "What is this, most likely?" — not where it is in the image, not how many there are, just what it is overall.

This is the simplest computer vision task, and it's the foundation everything else builds on — including object detection and transfer learning, which we'll cover in Parts 3 and 4.

🧩 Anatomy of a simple CNN

A basic image classification CNN is made of a handful of layer types, stacked in sequence. Each one transforms the data a little more, until what's left is easy to turn into a decision.

1

Convolution

Small filters slide across the image, detecting simple patterns like edges, corners, and textures.

2

Activation (ReLU)

Adds non-linearity so the network can learn complex patterns, not just straight lines.

3

Pooling

Shrinks the feature map, keeping the strongest signals and discarding redundant detail.

4

Flatten

Converts the final 2D feature maps into a single 1D vector of numbers.

5

Fully Connected

Combines all the extracted features to weigh evidence for each possible class.

6

Softmax

Converts raw scores into probabilities that sum to 100% across all classes.

Convolution and pooling layers are usually stacked several times in a row before flattening — early layers detect simple things like edges, and deeper layers combine them into more complex shapes like ears, eyes, or fur texture.

🐱 Walkthrough: classifying cats vs. dogs

What each stage "sees"

Input: A 128×128 pixel color photo — really just a grid of numbers, 3 channels deep (red, green, blue).

Early conv layers: Detect edges, curves, and color contrasts — the outline of an ear, the boundary between fur and background.

Deeper conv layers: Combine those edges into textures and shapes — pointed ears, whisker patterns, snout shape.

Fully connected layer: Weighs the evidence: "pointed ears + whiskers + this fur texture" leans strongly toward "cat."

Softmax output: Something like cat: 0.91, dog: 0.09 — the model is 91% confident this is a cat.

Notice that the network never explicitly learns the concept of "ears" — it discovers useful patterns on its own during training, purely because they help it tell classes apart.

🔁 How does it learn those filters?

At first, the convolution filters are just random numbers — they detect nothing meaningful. Training is the process of adjusting them so they become useful edge, texture, and shape detectors.

The short version: the model makes a prediction, we measure how wrong it was with a loss function, and backpropagation nudges every filter's values slightly to reduce that error. Repeat this thousands of times across thousands of images, and the filters gradually specialize into exactly the pattern detectors a cat-vs-dog classifier needs.

We cover activation functions and the training loop in more depth in the AI & Signal Processing Essentials series — worth a look if you want the underlying math.

⚖️ Simple CNN vs. traditional feature engineering

ApproachHow features are foundTrade-off
Traditional ML (e.g. SVM + HOG)Hand-designed by a human expertInterpretable, but brittle — fails on unseen variation
Simple CNNLearned automatically from dataMore robust, but needs more data and compute

This is the core shift that made deep learning dominant in computer vision: instead of a human deciding what patterns matter, the network figures it out from examples.

💡 Final takeaway

A simple CNN turns pixels into predictions through a repeatable pipeline: convolution finds patterns, pooling condenses them, and fully connected layers turn the evidence into a decision. Nothing is hand-coded — every filter is learned. In Part 3, we'll go beyond "what is in this image" to "where is it" with object detection and YOLO.
