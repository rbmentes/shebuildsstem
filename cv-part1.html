How Computers "See" — Pixels to Predictions

A gentle introduction to computer vision, for absolute beginners 👁️

When you look at a photo of a cat, your brain instantly knows it's a cat. No effort, no counting, no math — just recognition. But a computer doesn't have eyes, or a brain that "understands" images the way we do. So how does it work at all?

The answer starts with one simple idea: to a computer, an image is just a big grid of numbers. That's it. Once you accept that, everything else in computer vision starts to make sense.

🖼️ An Image Is a Grid of Numbers

Every image is made of tiny squares called pixels. A small image might be 28×28 pixels — that's 784 individual squares. Each pixel holds a number that represents how bright or dark it is.

For a grayscale image, each pixel is a single number between 0 and 255:

  • 0 = completely black
  • 255 = completely white
  • Everything in between = a shade of gray

That diagonal gradient you see on the left looks smooth to your eye — but to a computer, it's literally just the grid of numbers on the right. A neural network never "sees" the image at all. It only ever works with this matrix of numbers.

🌈 What About Color Images?

A color image works the same way, but instead of one grid, it has three — one each for Red, Green, and Blue (RGB). Stack these three grids on top of each other, and you get every color your screen can display.

So a small color photo that's 28×28 pixels isn't really one grid of 784 numbers — it's three grids, giving 28 × 28 × 3 = 2,352 numbers total. A regular smartphone photo (around 3000×4000 pixels) contains 36 million numbers. That's the scale computer vision works at.

💡 Why this matters: Once an image becomes "just numbers," it becomes something a computer can do math on — add, multiply, compare, transform. That's the entire foundation computer vision is built on.

🧩 So How Does "Seeing" Happen?

If an image is just numbers, how does a model go from a giant grid of brightness values to "this is a cat"? The short answer: patterns.

Early computer vision researchers tried to hand-code rules — "if there are two dark circular regions near the top, it might be eyes." This worked poorly. Real images are messy: different lighting, angles, backgrounds, and breeds of cat all look different in pixel values, even though they're all obviously "cat" to us.

The breakthrough came from letting the computer learn the patterns itself, rather than hand-coding them. This is where machine learning — and specifically a type of neural network called a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) — comes in. Instead of looking at the whole image at once, a CNN slides small filters across the image, learning to detect simple things first:

  • Edges — where brightness changes sharply
  • Textures — fur, fabric, smooth surfaces
  • Shapes — curves, corners, circles
  • Objects — combining shapes into "ear," "eye," "whisker"
  • Concepts — combining objects into "cat"

Each layer of the network builds on the last — simple patterns combine into more complex ones, until the final layer can confidently say "cat" or "not cat." We'll build exactly this kind of network from scratch in Part 2.

🔑 Key Takeaways

  • An image is just a grid (or three grids, for color) of numbers representing brightness.
  • Computers don't "see" — they do math on these number grids.
  • Hand-coded rules don't generalize well to messy real-world images.
  • CNNs learn to detect patterns layer by layer: edges → textures → shapes → objects.
