💪 EMG & Bioelectrical Signals
Part 2

Reading the Signal — Hardware, Electrodes and Noise

Biomedical Signal Processing · Electrode Systems · Signal Acquisition

In Part 1 we learned what EMG is and how muscles generate electrical signals. Now the engineering question: how do we actually capture those signals? EMG recording is a hardware problem before it becomes a software problem — and the choices you make at the acquisition stage determine everything that comes after.

📡 Types of EMG electrodes

There are two main ways to record EMG, and they serve completely different purposes:

🔵

Surface EMG (sEMG)

Electrodes placed on the skin above the muscle. Non-invasive, easy to apply, and perfect for gesture recognition and prosthetics research. Records the combined activity of multiple motor units.

Non-invasive · Research & clinical
📍

Needle EMG (iEMG)

A fine needle electrode inserted directly into the muscle tissue. Highly specific — can isolate single motor unit activity. Used in clinical diagnosis of neuromuscular diseases.

Invasive · Clinical diagnosis only

For AI and gesture recognition applications — including the project we built in this series — surface EMG is always used. It's non-invasive, wearable, and can be recorded continuously without discomfort.

🔧 The EMG acquisition hardware chain

From skin to digital data, every EMG system goes through the same chain of hardware components. Understanding each stage is fundamental EE knowledge:

1

Differential electrodes

Two electrodes placed over the muscle belly plus one reference electrode (usually over a bony landmark). The differential configuration records the voltage difference between the two active electrodes — this is crucial for rejecting common-mode noise like power line interference.

AgCl electrodes most common
2

Instrumentation amplifier

The raw EMG signal is only 0.1–5 mV — far too small for an ADC to measure accurately. An instrumentation amplifier with high CMRR (Common Mode Rejection Ratio) amplifies the differential signal typically by 1000x while rejecting common-mode interference.

Gain: ×1000 · CMRR > 80 dB
3

Bandpass filter (analog)

A hardware bandpass filter (20–500 Hz) removes DC offset, motion artifacts below 20 Hz, and high-frequency interference above 500 Hz. This is done in analog before digitization to prevent aliasing.

20 – 500 Hz passband
4

Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC)

Converts the continuous analog signal into discrete digital samples. The sampling rate must satisfy Nyquist's theorem — at least 2× the highest frequency of interest.

1000 – 2000 Hz sampling rate · 12-16 bit resolution
5

Digital processing

The digitized samples are now ready for software processing — additional filtering, feature extraction, and machine learning classification. This is where Python takes over.

Microcontroller or PC · Python / MATLAB

📐 Sampling rate — why it matters

By the Nyquist theorem, your sampling rate must be at least twice the highest frequency in the signal. EMG contains frequencies up to 500 Hz, so you need at least 1000 Hz. In practice most systems use 1000–2000 Hz:

📍 Electrode placement rules

Where you place the electrodes directly affects signal quality. Poor placement is one of the most common sources of bad EMG data:

Surface EMG electrode placement guidelines
Place electrodes over the muscle belly — the thickest part of the muscle, midway between origin and insertion
Align electrodes parallel to the muscle fiber direction — this maximises signal amplitude
Keep inter-electrode distance 20mm — standard for most surface EMG applications
Place the reference electrode over a bony prominence (e.g. elbow epicondyle) with no underlying muscle
Clean and lightly abrade the skin before application — reduces skin-electrode impedance dramatically
Avoid electrode placement over motor point — can cause signal saturation during strong contractions

⚠️ Sources of noise in EMG

Raw EMG is notoriously noisy. Before any processing, you need to understand what the noise sources are so you can remove them appropriately:

50/60 Hz

Power line interference

Electromagnetic interference from AC power lines. Appears as a sharp 50 Hz (Europe) or 60 Hz (USA) spike in the frequency spectrum. Removed with a notch filter.

Motion

Motion artifacts

Low-frequency noise (below 20 Hz) caused by electrode movement relative to the skin. Removed by the high-pass cutoff of the bandpass filter.

Crosstalk

Muscle crosstalk

Signal from adjacent muscles bleeding into your recording. Especially problematic in forearm recordings where muscles are densely packed. Reduced by careful electrode placement.

Electrode

Electrode-skin impedance noise

Poor electrode-skin contact creates high impedance which amplifies thermal noise. Reduced by proper skin preparation — cleaning, light abrasion, and conductive gel.

Amplifier

Amplifier thermal noise

Inherent electronic noise from the amplifier circuit. Unavoidable but minimised by using low-noise instrumentation amplifiers with high CMRR.

💡 Key takeaways from Part 2

Recording EMG well is an engineering challenge before it's a data science challenge. The electrode type, placement, amplifier quality, and sampling rate all determine the quality of data that reaches your algorithm.
