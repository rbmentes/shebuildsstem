💪 EMG & Bioelectrical Signals
Part 1

What Is EMG? The Electrical Language of Muscles

Biomedical Signal Processing · Electromyography · EE Fundamentals

Every time you move your hand, clench your fist, or type on a keyboard, your muscles are generating tiny electrical signals. These signals can be measured, recorded, and — with the right AI — decoded to understand your intentions before you even complete a movement. That's what electromyography (EMG) is all about, and it's what we'll explore in this series.

🧬 What is EMG?

Electromyography (EMG) is the measurement of electrical activity produced by skeletal muscles. When a muscle fiber contracts, it generates an electrical potential — a tiny voltage change — that can be detected by electrodes placed on or near the muscle. The recorded signal is called an electromyogram.

The word comes from Greek: electro (electrical) + myo (muscle) + graphy (recording). Simply put — EMG is how we listen to muscles using electricity.

⚡ How muscles generate electrical signals

To understand EMG we need to understand what actually happens when you decide to move. It starts in your brain and ends in your muscle fibers — a cascade of electrical events:

🧠Brain sends command
→
⚡Action potential travels down motor nerve
→
🔗Neuromuscular junction activates
→
💪Muscle fibers depolarize and contract
→
📡Electrical field detected by electrode

🔬 The motor unit — the basic unit of EMG

EMG doesn't measure a single muscle fiber. It measures the combined activity of motor units — each one consisting of a motor neuron and all the muscle fibers it controls. When a motor unit fires, all its fibers contract together, creating a detectable electrical spike called a Motor Unit Action Potential (MUAP).

From brain command to EMG signal
🧠

Motor cortex

Sends a voluntary command — "close the fist." The signal travels down the spinal cord.

⚡

Motor neuron

Carries the action potential from the spinal cord to the muscle. Travels at up to 70 m/s.

💪

Muscle fibers

Depolarize and contract together. Multiple motor units fire at different times creating complex patterns.

📊

EMG signal

The sum of all active motor unit action potentials — recorded as a voltage vs time waveform.

📊 What does an EMG signal look like?

A raw EMG signal is a complex, rapidly oscillating waveform. At rest, it's nearly flat. During contraction, it bursts into high-frequency activity. The amplitude and frequency content both carry information about which muscles are active and how hard they're working.

Typical surface EMG signal — rest vs contraction

Key characteristics of a surface EMG signal:

📏
Amplitude
0.1 – 5 mV (very small!)
🎵
Frequency range
20 – 500 Hz
⏱️
Sampling rate
1000 – 2000 Hz minimum

🔌 The EE perspective

⚡ EMG as an electrical engineering problem

From an EE standpoint, the EMG electrode is a transducer — it converts an ionic electrical signal in biological tissue into an electronic voltage that a circuit can measure. The signal is then amplified (often ×1000), filtered to remove interference, and digitized by an ADC. Understanding this chain — transducer → amplifier → filter → ADC — is fundamental to building any biosignal acquisition system.

💡 Why does EMG matter for AI?

EMG is one of the few signals that directly encodes motor intent — what movement you are about to make, before you finish making it. This makes it uniquely valuable for:

🦾

Prosthetic limbs

Amputees control robotic hands using EMG from residual arm muscles

🎮

Gesture control

Control computers, games, or robots with hand gestures — no camera needed

🏋️

Sports science

Analyse muscle activation patterns to optimize athletic performance

🏥

Clinical diagnosis

Detect neuromuscular diseases like ALS, muscular dystrophy, carpal tunnel

🤖

Human-robot interaction

Real-time robot control that responds to human muscle signals

🧘

Rehabilitation

Biofeedback therapy for stroke recovery and physical rehabilitation

💡 Key takeaways from Part 1

EMG is the electrical signal your muscles produce when they contract — generated by motor unit action potentials, detected by electrodes, and amplified into a measurable waveform. It encodes motor intent, meaning it tells you what someone is trying to do before the movement is complete. That's what makes it so powerful for AI-driven applications like prosthetics and gesture control.
